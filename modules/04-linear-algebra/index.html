04 · Linear Algebra Essentials

Modules 01–03 taught you to write Python. This module teaches you the language the rest of the course is written in. Every dataset you will ever load is a grid of numbers; every model you will ever train turns that grid into predictions by multiplying it by another grid. A linear regression, a neural network layer, and an attention head are the same operation at different scales — and that operation is the one you are about to learn by hand, then draw, then implement, then hand to NumPy. There is no calculus here and no prerequisite beyond arithmetic: if you can multiply and add, you can do all of it.

Before any definitions, look at something you already understand: a spreadsheet.

Strip away the labels and what remains is a grid of ten numbers, five rows by two columns. That grid is a matrix. In machine learning it has a standard name — X, the data matrix — and a standard shape convention that never changes:

The convention that holds for the rest of the course

One row per example, one column per feature. Row i is everything you know about the i-th flower, customer, image, or sentence. Column j is one measurement taken across every example. When you later write model.fit(X, y), X is exactly this grid, and the very first thing that goes wrong for beginners is passing it in the other orientation.

Written as a matrix, with the labels dropped and brackets added, our five flowers are:

X 1.40.2 4.71.4 5.11.9 1.30.4 4.51.5 → shape 5 × 2

The shape is always written rows-first: 5 × 2 means five rows and two columns. Say it out loud as "five by two" every time you meet one, because in Part 6 the shape is what tells you whether a multiplication is even legal.

Four words you now need: scalar, vector, matrix, tensor

These are not four unrelated things. They are the same thing — a box of numbers — counted by how many indices you need to reach a single number inside it.

NameWhat it isIndices to reach one numberExample from the tableNumPy
Scalar A single number None 1.4 — flower A's petal length 1.4
Vector An ordered list of numbers One — which entry (1.4, 0.2) — all of flower A np.array([1.4, 0.2]), shape (2,)
Matrix A grid of numbers Two — row and column The whole table np.array([[1.4, 0.2], ...]), shape (5, 2)
Tensor The library term for a multidimensional array Zero or more 100 colour photos: 100 × 64 × 64 × 3 np.zeros((100, 64, 64, 3))

"Tensor" sounds intimidating and is not. In libraries such as PyTorch it is the umbrella term: a scalar is a 0-axis tensor, a vector has 1 axis, a matrix has 2, and higher-rank tensors have 3 or more. A batch of colour images has 4 axes — image, row, column, and channel. Module 32 introduces tensor code; Module 33 uses higher-rank image tensors. Everything computed in this module is a scalar, vector, or matrix.

The second reading: a vector is an arrow

Here is the move that turns arithmetic into geometry, and it is the single most valuable idea in the module. Flower A is the pair (1.4, 0.2). That pair is a list of two numbers — but it is also an instruction: go 1.4 to the right, then 0.2 up. Follow the instruction from the origin and you land on a point. Draw an arrow from the origin to that point, and you have drawn the vector.

Reading 1 · a list

(1.4, 0.2) is two numbers in a fixed order. This reading is right when you are computing, storing, or indexing — v[0] is the petal length.

Reading 2 · a point

The same pair names a location on a plane. This reading is right when you are thinking about distance, neighbours, or clusters — the whole of Module 18 (k-NN) lives here.

Reading 3 · an arrow

An arrow from the origin to that point, with a length and a direction. This reading is right when you are adding, scaling, or transforming — most of this module lives here.

They are three views of one object, and fluent people switch between them mid-sentence without noticing. When a piece of linear algebra confuses you, the fix is almost always to switch reading: if the algebra is opaque, draw the arrow; if the picture is ambiguous, write the list.

Common pitfall · letters get reused, constantly

Mathematics has 26 letters and rather more ideas, so the same letter means different things in different places. In this module alone, a and b are sometimes the coefficients in a combination, sometimes entries of a matrix, and sometimes whole points; b is also the bias in Wx + b and the right-hand side of Ax = b. Capital Σ is the summation sign here and the name of a matrix in Part 9. The dot · means "dot product" between two vectors and simply "apply" or "multiply" between matrices.

This is not sloppiness you need to fix, and it is not something to memorise your way around. Read the type of the thing, not the letter. If both sides are vectors, · is a dot product. If one side is a matrix, it is multiplication. Experienced readers do this without noticing; you will too, sooner than you expect.

That last row is worth dwelling on, because Σ frightens people out of proportion to its difficulty. These two lines say the same thing:

Common pitfall · the word "dimension" means three different things

This one genuinely trips up beginners, and the confusion is nobody's fault: the word is overloaded.

  1. The dimension of a vector — how many numbers are in it. (1.4, 0.2) is 2-dimensional. Some text embeddings are 1536-dimensional; each is still just one vector, a list of 1536 numbers.
  2. The number of axes of an array — what NumPy calls ndim. A vector has ndim == 1 no matter how long it is. So a 1536-dimensional vector has ndim == 1. Both statements are correct, and they are about different things.
  3. The dimension of a vector space — how many independent basis directions it contains. For a matrix, rank is the dimension of its column space: the independent output directions that survive the transformation.

When someone says "high-dimensional data" they mean sense 1: many features per example. When NumPy says ndim, it means sense 2. Read shape, not ndim, when you want to know how big something is.

Common pitfall · maths indexes from 1, Python indexes from 0

A textbook's v₁ is Python's v[0]. A textbook's A₃₂ is A[2, 1]. Nothing you can do will make these agree, so build the habit now: when you transcribe a formula into code, subtract one from every index, deliberately and out loud. The bug this causes is silent — the code runs and gives a wrong answer — which makes it much worse than a crash.

Vectors: adding, scaling, and what you can reach

There are exactly two things you can do to vectors on their own: add them and stretch them. That is not an oversimplification for beginners — it is the actual definition. A vector space is a set of things you can add and stretch. Everything else in this module is built from those two operations, so it is worth getting both pictures firmly in mind.

Adding: walk one, then walk the other

In numbers, addition is boring: line the vectors up and add matching entries.

31 + 12 = 3+11+2 = 43

In pictures it is much more interesting. Treat each vector as a walking instruction. To compute u + v: start at the origin, walk along u, and then — without turning round — walk along v from wherever you landed. Where you end up is the sum. This is called tip-to-tail addition.

Two consequences fall straight out of that picture, and both are worth noticing rather than memorising:

  • Order does not matter. Walking u then v lands you in the same place as walking v then u — the two routes are the two sides of a parallelogram, and both reach the far corner. So u + v = v + u. (Hold on to your surprise here: in Part 6 you will meet matrix multiplication, where order does matter. The contrast is the point.)
  • A vector has no fixed home. When we slide v so its tail sits on u's tip, it is still the same vector — same length, same direction. A vector is a displacement, not a location. We usually draw it from the origin out of convention, and because that lets us treat "the vector (3,1)" and "the point (3,1)" as interchangeable.

Subtracting: the arrow from one point to another

Subtraction is component-wise too — u − v = (u₁ − v₁, u₂ − v₂) — but its picture is the one people forget, and it is the one you will use most often in machine learning.

The one idea

b − a is the arrow that points from a to b. Not from the origin — from a to b. Check it against the tip-to-tail picture: if you start at a and add b − a, you arrive at b, because a + (b − a) = b.

Switch Demo 2 above into subtraction mode and watch this happen. It is the picture people forget, and the one you will use most.

This is why the distance between two data points is ‖b − a‖ — the length of the arrow between them, which is Part 3. It is also why the gap between what a model predicted and what actually happened gets written y − ŷ: it is the arrow from the prediction to the truth, and its length is how wrong you were. (That gap has a name — the residual — and Part 8 is largely about making it as short as possible. The hat on ŷ means "our estimate", not "length 1".) Subtraction is how you turn two locations into a comparison.

Scaling: same line, different length

Multiplying a vector by a plain number — a scalar — multiplies every entry: 2 · (3, 1) = (6, 2). The picture is a stretch along the same line:

Scalar cWhat happens to the arrow
c > 1Stretches longer, same direction.
c = 1Nothing at all.
0 < c < 1Shrinks toward the origin, same direction.
c = 0Collapses to the origin — the zero vector, which has no direction.
c < 0Flips to point the opposite way, and scales by |c|.

The word "scalar" now makes sense: it is a number whose job is to scale. Notice what scaling can never do — it cannot move an arrow off the line it already lies on. Every multiple of (3, 1) lands somewhere on the infinite straight line through the origin and (3, 1). To leave that line you need a second, different vector, which is the next idea.

Linear combinations and span: where can you get to?

Put the two operations together and you get the single most important expression in the subject:

a u  +  b v

Scale u by some amount, scale v by some amount, add the results. That is a linear combination of u and v. The entire rest of this module is linear combinations wearing different hats: a matrix–vector product is a linear combination of the matrix's columns; a neural network layer is a linear combination of its inputs; a prediction is a linear combination of features weighted by what the model learned.

Now ask the natural question: as a and b range over every possible number, which points can you reach? That set is called the span of u and v, and for two vectors in the plane there are exactly three answers.

Hold on to that word "redundant" — in Part 7 it becomes rank, and in Module 13 it becomes the reason two duplicate features make a regression model unstable.

Basis: why coordinates are coefficients

Two special vectors get their own names because everything is secretly written in terms of them:

î (i-hat)10 ĵ (j-hat)01

One step right, and one step up. Together they are the standard basis. And now notice something that has been hiding in plain sight since Part 1:

31 = 3 · î  +  1 · ĵ

The coordinates of a vector are precisely the coefficients of the linear combination that builds it out of î and ĵ. Writing (3, 1) has always meant "three steps right plus one step up" — we just never said it in those words. A basis is any pair of vectors independent enough to build everything this way, and the standard basis is merely the most convenient choice, not the only one.

This reframing does real work later. In Part 5 you will see that a matrix is fully described by where it sends î and ĵ — four numbers, two destinations. In Module 25, PCA will choose a different basis, aligned with the directions your data actually varies in, and "changing basis" will just mean re-describing the same points with better-chosen coefficients.

Where this shows up in machine learning

  • Every linear prediction is a linear combination. A model that predicts house price as w₁·(size) + w₂·(bedrooms) + w₃·(age) is combining feature vectors with learned coefficients. "Training" means searching for the coefficients — Modules 11 and 12.
  • Gradient descent is repeated vector addition. Training a model means holding a vector of parameters, working out a direction that would reduce the error, scaling that direction down to a small step, and adding it on. Then doing it again, a few thousand times. Every training loop you will ever write is Demo 2 on repeat — Module 05 shows how the direction is found.
  • Word vectors do arithmetic. Modern language models represent each word as a vector — an embedding — positioned so that words used in similar ways end up close together. The famous king − man + woman ≈ queen is exactly the subtraction and addition on this page: king − man is the arrow from "man" to "king", and adding it to "woman" walks the same displacement from a different starting point. Reveal the answers

1. (0, 0), the zero vector. v is −1 · u: same line, opposite direction, same length. Walking u then v brings you exactly back to where you started.

2. (4, 1) − (1, 5) = (3, −4). Destination minus start — and if you write it the other way round you get the arrow pointing back. Getting this order backwards is the single most common slip in this part; check it by asking "does start + arrow land on destination?"

3. Yes for (6, 4), because it is 2 · (3, 2) — on the line. No for (6, 5): doubling gives (6, 4), so no single scalar produces a 6 in the first slot and a 5 in the second. One vector spans only its own line.

4. A line. v = −3 · u, so v adds nothing new — they are linearly dependent. Every combination a·u + b·v equals (a − 3b)·u, which is still just a multiple of u.

5. Yes: −7 · î + 2.5 · ĵ. The standard basis reaches every point in the plane, and the coefficients are simply the coordinates you were handed. That is the whole point of a basis.

Length, distance, and direction

An arrow has two properties: how long it is, and which way it points. Part 2 was about direction. This part is about length — and length turns out to be how machine learning measures error, similarity, and model complexity. Three of the most important quantities in the field are all "the length of some vector".

The length of a vector is Pythagoras

Draw the vector (3, 4). Drop a line straight down to the x-axis. You now have a right triangle with legs 3 and 4, and the vector is its hypotenuse. So its length is √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5. That is the entire derivation — the formula you are about to meet is Pythagoras's theorem with new notation.

Key formula · the L2 norm (ordinary length)

For a vector v with entries v₁, v₂, …, vd:

‖v‖ = √(v₁² + v₂² + … + v_d²)

Read ‖v‖ as "the norm of v" or just "the length of v". Squaring makes every term positive (so opposite directions do not cancel), and the square root undoes the squaring so the answer comes back in the original units. Written with sigma: ‖v‖ = √(Σᵢ vᵢ²) — a loop that accumulates squares, then one square root at the end.

The double bars are there to distinguish it from |x|, the absolute value of a single number. In fact ‖v‖ for a 1-entry vector is the absolute value, so it is a genuine generalisation of "how big is this" from numbers to lists of numbers.

It also works unchanged in any number of dimensions. The length of the 4-dimensional vector (1, 2, 2, 4) is √(1 + 4 + 4 + 16) = √25 = 5. You cannot draw it, but the arithmetic does not care — and this is the general pattern of the whole subject: the pictures stop at 3 dimensions, the formulas never stop. Draw in 2-D to build intuition, then trust the algebra in 300-D.

Distance is the length of the difference

Part 2 established that b − a is the arrow from a to b. Its length is therefore the distance between the two points:

Key formula · Euclidean distance

distance(a, b) = ‖b − a‖ = √((b₁−a₁)² + (b₂−a₂)² + … )

Subtract first, then take the length. Note that ‖b − a‖ = ‖a − b‖: the two arrows point opposite ways but have the same length, so distance is symmetric, as it should be.

Back to Demo 1's flowers. Flower A was (1.4, 0.2) and flower D was (1.3, 0.4). Their difference is (−0.1, 0.2), whose length is √(0.01 + 0.04) ≈ 0.224. Flower B, at (4.7, 1.4), is √(3.3² + 1.2²) ≈ 3.51 away from A — more than fifteen times further. That number, computed for every pair, is literally all that the k-nearest-neighbours classifier in Module 18 does.

Unit vectors: keeping direction, discarding length

Sometimes you want only the direction. Divide a vector by its own length and you get a vector of length exactly 1 pointing the same way — a unit vector, usually written with a hat: v̂ = v / ‖v‖. This is called normalising.

For v = (3, 4), whose length is 5: v̂ = (3/5, 4/5) = (0.6, 0.8). Check it — √(0.36 + 0.64) = √1 = 1. It points in precisely the same direction, but now it is a pure statement of direction with the magnitude divided out.

This matters more than it looks. Two documents about the same topic, one 100 words and one 10,000 words, have embedding vectors pointing in nearly the same direction but with wildly different lengths. Compare them by distance and the long one looks like an outlier; normalise first and they sit right next to each other. Length often encodes something you do not care about — and normalising is how you throw it away deliberately.

A second kind of length: the L1 norm

"Length" is a choice, not a law. The L2 norm takes the straight-line route. The L1 norm takes the route a taxi drives on a street grid — along one axis, then the other:

Key formula · the L1 norm

‖v‖₁ = |v₁| + |v₂| + … + |v_d|

Add up the absolute values. For (3, 4): 3 + 4 = 7, compared with L2's 5. The L1 distance is never shorter than the L2 distance, because the straight line is the shortest path.

The clearest way to see the difference is to draw every vector of length exactly 1 under each definition — the unit ball. Under L2 it is a circle. Under L1 it is a diamond with corners on the axes, which you can confirm in Demo 4. That diamond shape is not a curiosity: its sharp corners sit exactly on the axes, where one coordinate is zero, and that turns out to be the reason two closely-related methods behave differently. Both Ridge and Lasso (Module 13) improve a model by insisting its weight vector stays short — Ridge measures "short" with L2, Lasso with L1. Because L1's ball has corners sitting on the axes, Lasso tends to push weights to exactly zero, switching features off entirely, while Ridge only shrinks them towards zero. The shape of the ball decides the behaviour of the model. When you reach that module, come back and look at this demo again.

Common pitfalls with norms

  • Forgetting the square root. v₁² + v₂² is the squared norm, ‖v‖², not the norm. Squared norms appear constantly in ML (they are easier to differentiate, as Module 05 will show) so both are useful — but they are different numbers and mixing them up silently corrupts a result. Mean squared error is a squared norm; root mean squared error takes the root back off.
  • Normalising the zero vector. v / ‖v‖ divides by zero when v is all zeros. NumPy returns nan rather than crashing, and nan then spreads silently through every later calculation. Guard it.
  • Assuming distance means dissimilarity. Two vectors can point in exactly the same direction and still be far apart, if one is much longer. Whether that counts as "different" depends entirely on your problem — which is what Part 4's cosine similarity exists to address.
  • Comparing distances across unscaled features. If one column is measured in metres (range 0–2) and another in millimetres (range 0–2000), the millimetre column dominates every distance and the metre column is effectively ignored. This is why any method built on distances needs feature scaling first — rescaling every column to a comparable range so that no feature dominates by accident of units. It is a genuine, common, silent bug, not a technicality.

Where this shows up in machine learning

  • Loss functions. Mean squared error is ‖y − ŷ‖² / n — the squared length of the error vector. Mean absolute error is the L1 version. Choosing a loss is choosing which norm measures your mistakes.
  • Regularisation. Ridge penalises ‖w‖², Lasso penalises ‖w‖₁. Both say "keep the weight vector short" — they just disagree about what short means, and that disagreement is the diamond versus the circle. Module 13.
  • k-nearest neighbours and k-means. Both are built entirely on ‖b − a‖. Modules 18 and 24.
  • Normalised embeddings. Sentence-transformer libraries can normalise output vectors (for example, with normalize_embeddings=True), so that comparing them by dot product is comparing them by angle. Module 42.
  • Gradient clipping. When training blows up, the standard fix is to measure the length of the step the model wants to take and shrink it if it exceeds a threshold. A norm, used as a safety valve. Module 31.

The dot product: the most useful operation in ML

If you learn one thing in this module and forget the rest, learn this one. The dot product takes two vectors and returns a single number measuring how much they agree. A prediction from a linear model is a dot product. A single artificial neuron's output is a dot product. When a language model decides how much one word in a sentence should pay attention to another, the score it uses is a dot product. When a search engine ranks documents against your query, it ranks them by a dot product. It is the same small operation every time, at wildly different scales.

Definition one: multiply matching entries and add

Key formula · the dot product

u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + … + u_d v_d  =  Σᵢ uᵢvᵢ

Pair up matching entries, multiply each pair, add all the products. The result is a scalar — a single number, not a vector. In NumPy this is u @ v or np.dot(u, v).

A worked example, small enough to do in your head:

u = (3, 1)    v = (1, 2)

u · v = 3×1 + 1×2
      = 3 + 2
      = 5

That is genuinely all of the arithmetic. What makes it interesting is what the number means, and for that you need to notice its sign.

The sign is the message

If u · v is…Then the two nonzero arrows…Angle between them
Large and positivePoint in broadly the same direction — and both are reasonably long, since the two lengths multiply togetherLess than 90°
Exactly zeroAre at right angles — orthogonalExactly 90° (provided neither vector is zero)
NegativePoint in broadly opposite directionsMore than 90°

"Orthogonal" means geometrically perpendicular: the dot product is zero. It does not by itself mean statistical independence. In PCA, choosing orthonormal eigenvectors of the covariance matrix makes the resulting component scores uncorrelated, so they do not repeat the same linear variation; they can still have nonlinear dependence.

Definition two: length times length times alignment

There is a second formula for the same number, and it is the one that explains the first:

Key formula · the geometric form

u · v = ‖u‖ · ‖v‖ · cos(θ)

where θ is the angle between the two arrows. You do not need trigonometry to use this. You only need one fact about cos(θ): it is a dial that runs from +1 to −1, reading +1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 when they are perpendicular, and −1 when they point opposite ways. It is a pure alignment score.

So the dot product is three things multiplied together: how long u is, how long v is, and how aligned they are. Every part of the table above follows immediately — the sign of the dot product is the sign of cos(θ), because lengths are never negative.

Why do the two definitions agree? The short version, which is enough for now: the dot product measures how much of one vector points along the other. Shine a light straight down onto the line through v. The shadow u casts on that line is called the projection of u onto v. The signed amount of u along v is ‖u‖cos(θ): positive in v's direction and negative in the opposite direction. Its absolute value is the shadow's geometric length. Multiplying the signed component by ‖v‖ gives the dot product.

Key formula · projection

The signed scalar component of u along nonzero v, followed by its geometric length:

signed component = (u · v) / ‖v‖
shadow length    = |u · v| / ‖v‖

The vector that shadow points to:

proj_v(u) = ((u · v) / (v · v)) · v

Read the second one as: "compute a number, then scale v by it." The result always lies on v's line, because it is a multiple of v. This single formula reappears in Part 8 as the entire geometric content of least squares.

Worked, projecting u = (4, 3) onto v = (1, 0):

u · v = 4×1 + 3×0 = 4
v · v = 1×1 + 0×0 = 1

proj  = (4 / 1) · (1, 0) = (4, 0)

And that had to be the answer: v is the x-axis direction, so projecting onto it means "keep the x coordinate, discard the y". Projection is deliberate, controlled information loss — which is exactly how Module 25 will use it.

Cosine similarity: comparing direction only

Rearrange the geometric formula to solve for the alignment dial on its own:

Key formula · cosine similarity

cos(θ) = (u · v) / (‖u‖ · ‖v‖)

For two nonzero vectors, take the dot product and divide out both lengths. What remains is pure alignment between −1 and +1: 1 means identical direction, 0 means perpendicular, and −1 means opposite. A zero vector has no direction, so its cosine similarity and angle are undefined.

Worked, for u = (1, 0) and v = (1, 1):

u · v  = 1×1 + 0×1 = 1
‖u‖    = √(1 + 0) = 1
‖v‖    = √(1 + 1) = √2 ≈ 1.414

cos(θ) = 1 / (1 × 1.414) ≈ 0.707

A calculator will tell you that cos 45° ≈ 0.707, and 45° is exactly the angle between "straight along the x-axis" and "diagonally up and right" — so the arithmetic and the picture agree. You will rarely need to convert back to degrees; the number between −1 and 1 is usually the thing you actually want.

Why divide the lengths out? Because length usually encodes something irrelevant. Recall the 100-word and 10,000-word documents from Part 3: they discuss the same topic, so their vectors point the same way, but the long one's vector is much longer. Their raw dot product is large mostly because of length; their cosine similarity is near 1 because of topic. Cosine similarity is what you want whenever "more of the same thing" should not count as "different".

The bound between −1 and 1 is guaranteed and not a coincidence. Since cos(θ) can never leave that range, we get |u · v| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖ — the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, one of the genuinely important facts in mathematics, and here it falls out of a picture of a shadow.

Common pitfalls with dot products

  • Confusing it with element-wise multiplication. u · v = 5 is one number. u * v = (3, 2) — NumPy's * — is a vector of the same length as the inputs. Both are legal, both are useful, and NumPy will not warn you when you meant one and typed the other. Mnemonic: @ collapses, * preserves.
  • Reading a large dot product as "very similar". It might just mean "very long". Two nearly-perpendicular but huge vectors can out-score two short parallel ones. If you mean similarity, normalise — use cosine.
  • Comparing vectors of different lengths. u · v requires both to have the same number of entries. In NumPy this raises ValueError: shapes (3,) and (4,) not aligned, which is the good outcome: a loud failure.
  • Expecting orthogonal to mean "opposite". Orthogonal means perpendicular (dot product 0); opposite means the same line in reverse (cosine −1). These are different geometric situations, and confusing them will mislead you when reading similarity scores.

Where this shows up in machine learning

  • Every linear model's prediction. ŷ = w · x + b. The weight vector w and the feature vector x get dotted; that is the prediction. Training means finding the w most aligned with what predicts the target well. Modules 11–12.
  • A single neuron. output = activation(w · x + b) — the same dot product, with a nonlinearity wrapped round it. A layer of 128 neurons is 128 dot products, which is exactly one matrix–vector product (Part 5). Module 28.
  • Attention inside a language model. "How much should this word pay attention to that word" is answered by a dot product between two vectors, one standing for each word. The scores are then rescaled and converted into weights that add up to 1. Module 37.
  • Semantic search. A vector database stores one vector per document and answers your query by ranking them by cosine similarity to your query's vector. This is also how a chatbot looks up relevant documents before answering — retrieval-augmented generation, Module 42.
  • Correlation. Pearson correlation is the cosine similarity of two mean-centred feature vectors. The formula you will meet in Module 06 is the one on this page.

Matrices as functions on space

A matrix does two completely different jobs, and beginners are rarely told that they are different. Job one you met in Part 1: a matrix stores data — rows of examples, columns of features, an inert grid. Job two is the subject of this part: a matrix acts on vectors. It is a machine. You feed it a vector, and it hands back a different vector.

Same object, two roles. When you see X in model.fit(X, y), that is a data matrix. When you see W in Wx + b, that is a machine. The arithmetic is identical; only the intention differs. This part is entirely about the machine reading, because it is the one that gives you geometric intuition.

Matrix times vector: two ways to read the same arithmetic

Here is the operation. Take the matrix A and the vector x:

A 2013 x 21 → Ax 45

There are two correct stories about how those numbers were produced. Learn both. Nearly everyone is taught only the first, and the second is the one that makes linear algebra make sense.

The row view · "each output is a dot product"

Output entry i is row i of A dotted with x.

row 1: (2, 0) · (2, 1) = 4
row 2: (1, 3) · (2, 1) = 5

Best when you are computing, or thinking about a layer of neurons — each row is one neuron's weight vector, and each output is that neuron firing.

The column view · "the output is a combination of the columns"

The entries of x say how much of each column of A to take.

2 · (2, 1) + 1 · (0, 3)
  = (4, 2) + (0, 3)
  = (4, 5)

Best when you are understanding. This says Ax is a linear combination of A's columns — which links every matrix directly back to span.

Same answer, (4, 5), by two genuinely different routes. Now here is the payoff of the column view, which is the most important single sentence in this module:

The one idea

The columns of a matrix are where the basis vectors land. Feed î = (1, 0) into A and you get A's first column. Feed ĵ = (0, 1) and you get A's second column. So a matrix is nothing more than a record of two destinations — and because every other vector is a combination of î and ĵ, knowing those two destinations determines what happens to every point in the plane at once.

Check it against the numbers above. A's first column is (2, 1), so A sends î to (2, 1). Its second column is (0, 3), so it sends ĵ to (0, 3). And x = (2, 1) means "2 of î plus 1 of ĵ", so its destination must be "2 of î's destination plus 1 of ĵ's destination" — 2·(2,1) + 1·(0,3) = (4, 5). The column view is not a trick for computing; it is the definition of what the matrix is.

Shapes: what is legal, and what comes out


  
Matrix–vector multiplication only works when the shapes line up, and the rule is mechanical:


  

    A is m × n
    ×
    x has n entries
    →
    Ax has m entries
  


  
The matrix's column count must match the vector's length; the result's length is the
  matrix's row count. Read it as: n numbers go in, m numbers come out. So a
  128 × 784 matrix is a machine that takes a 784-number input and produces a 128-number
  output — which is exactly a neural network layer that reduces 784 pixels to 128 activations. The
  matrix does not have to be square, and when it is not, it changes the dimension of the space.


  

    
Common pitfalls with matrix–vector products

    
    
      
  • Multiplying in the wrong order. A @ x and x @ A
      are different operations with different shape requirements, and in NumPy both may run without
      error while producing different answers. When in doubt, write the shapes down first.
    • 
      
  • Applying a matrix to rows of data with X @ A versus
      A @ x. Because ML stores examples as rows, transforming a whole
      dataset is usually written X @ A.T, not A @ X. If a transformed
      scatter plot looks scrambled, this is the first thing to check.
    • 
      
  • Believing a matrix can shift things. It cannot: the origin is always
      fixed. Any operation that moves the origin needs a separate + b term. This is
      exactly why every neural network layer carries a bias vector.
    • 
      
  • Assuming rows are inputs. In Ax, the input's entries multiply
      A's columns. It is the columns that carry the "where does each input direction go"
      meaning.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Where this shows up in machine learning

    
A fully-connected neural network layer, in full, is:

    
h = activation(W @ x + b)

    
and you can now read every symbol. x is the input vector. W is a
    matrix whose rows are neurons' weight vectors — or equivalently, whose columns say where each
    input feature gets sent. W @ x is a stack of dot products, one per neuron.
    b shifts the result (the one thing a matrix cannot do on its own).
    activation bends the result, which is the only non-linear step and therefore the
    only reason stacking layers achieves anything — Module 29 explains why. Everything a deep network
    does, it does with the operation on this page, repeated.

Composing transformations: matrix × matrix


  
Part 5 gave you one machine. Now use two. Apply B to a vector, then apply
  A to the result. Is there a single matrix that does both in one step?


  
There is, and it is called AB. That is not a definition invented to be awkward — it
  is the reason matrix multiplication is defined the way it is. Everything odd about the
  rule, including why it is not element-wise and why order matters, follows from this one fact:


  

    
The one idea

    
AB means "do B first, then A." You read
    it right to left, like nested function calls: A(B(x)). Matrix products are
    composition of transformations, and that is the whole story.

  


  
The right-to-left order feels backwards the first time. It is inherited from function notation:
  f(g(x)) also applies g first. If it helps, read ABx as
  "A of B of x", moving inward from the right.


  
The shape rule, and why it is what it is


  
Not every pair of matrices can be multiplied. The rule:


  

    A is m × k
    ×
    B is k × n
    →
    AB is m × n
  


  
The inner numbers must match; the outer numbers survive. The reason is not
  arbitrary — it is the composition idea again. B takes an n-vector in and
  emits a k-vector. For A to accept that, A must take
  k numbers in. Its output is m numbers. So the combined machine takes
  n in and gives m out, which is exactly m × n. If the inner
  numbers disagree, the second machine cannot accept the first machine's output, and the product is
  genuinely meaningless — not merely disallowed.

Just as Ax had a row reading and a column reading, AB has four.
      All four give identical numbers; each makes a different fact obvious. This framing comes from
      Kenji Hiranabe's The Art of Linear Algebra, a set of graphic notes on
      Strang's teaching that is worth downloading.

      
    
        
  1. Entry by entry. Entry (i,j) is row i of A dotted
        with column j of B. This is the recipe above — best for computing by hand.
    2. 
        
  2. Column by column. Column j of AB is
        A applied to column j of B. So multiplying by A on the
        left transforms each of B's columns independently. Best for seeing that AB's
        columns live wherever A can send things.
    3. 
        
  3. Row by row. Row i of AB is row i of
        A times the whole of B. Best for seeing what happens when you
        multiply on the right.
    4.

        k, of (column k of A) × (row k of B) — each term being an
        outer product, a whole matrix built from one column and one row. This says every
        matrix product is a stack of simple layered patterns added together. It is the least
        obvious reading and by far the most consequential: it is the form behind image compression,
        PCA, recommender systems, and topic modelling. The same low-rank factorisation idea also

Common pitfalls with matrix multiplication

    
    
      
  • * is not @. In NumPy, A * B
      multiplies element by element; A @ B is matrix multiplication. Both are legal for
      two same-shaped square matrices, both return a matrix of that shape, and only one is what you
      meant. This is the most common silent numerical bug in beginner ML code.
    • 
      
  • A diagonal matrix on the left scales rows; on the right it scales
      columns. np.diag(d) @ A multiplies each row of A by the matching
      entry of d; A @ np.diag(d) multiplies each column. Both run,
      both look plausible, and they are different. When scaling features, you want the column
      version.
    • 
      
  • Transposing a product without reversing it. (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ, not
      AᵀBᵀ. Fortunately the wrong version usually has mismatched shapes and crashes,
      which is the kind fate.
    • 
      
  • Assuming you can cancel. From AB = AC you may
      not conclude B = C — not unless A is invertible, which is
      Part 7. If A destroys information, different inputs can produce
      identical outputs.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Where this shows up in machine learning

    
    
      
  • Why neural networks need activation functions. Stack two linear layers with
      nothing in between: W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x. By associativity, that is a
      single matrix. A hundred stacked linear layers collapse to one — so a deep linear
      network is exactly as expressive as a shallow one, which is to say, not very. The
      non-linearity between layers is what stops the collapse and is therefore the entire reason
      depth buys anything. Module 29.
    • 
      
  • Batching. Applying one transformation to n examples at once is a
      single matrix product: X @ W.T, with X of shape
      (n, d). One operation, n examples, and it runs on a GPU in parallel. This is why
      vectorised code is hundreds of times faster than a Python loop — Module 07.
    • 
      
  • Every pairwise similarity at once. If E holds
      n vectors as rows — one per word, say — then E @ E.T is the
      n × n grid of all pairwise dot products. One product computes every
      comparison. This is the shape of the attention step inside a language model: the
      QKᵀ you may have seen written down is a grid of dot products built exactly this
      way, from two learned re-projections of the same input rather than from one matrix, and then
      rescaled and turned into weights that sum to 1. The extra machinery is Module 37's business;
      the operation underneath it is this one.

This part answers one question — can the transformation be undone? — and the
  remarkable thing is that determinant, rank, invertibility, linear independence, and span all turn
  out to be different names for the answer. Five ideas, one picture. Most courses teach them as five
  separate chapters; you are going to learn them as one.


  
The determinant is an area scale factor


  
Take the unit square — area 1 — and apply a matrix. It becomes a parallelogram. The
  determinant is that parallelogram's area.


  

    
Key formula · the 2×2 determinant

    
det [[a, b],  =  ad − bc
     [c, d]]

    
That is the only determinant formula you need in this course. If det = 3, the
    matrix triples every area. If det = 0.5, it halves them. This works for any region,
    not just the unit square — a shape of area 7 becomes a shape of area 7 × |det|.

  


  
Three worked examples, small enough to do in your head. Cover the answers and try each first:


  
det [[3, 1],     = 3×4 − 1×2 = 12 − 2 = 10     areas ×10, orientation kept
     [2, 4]]

det [[1, 0],     = 1×0 − 0×0 = 0                everything flattened onto the x-axis
     [0, 0]]

det [[2, 4],     = 2×2 − 4×1 = 4 − 4 = 0        flattened — and not a zero entry in sight
     [1, 2]]


  
That third one is the case to remember. Every entry is a healthy nonzero number, and the matrix
  still destroys a dimension — because the second column, (4, 2), is exactly twice the
  first, (2, 1). Both columns point along one line, so everything the matrix can produce
  lands on that line. You cannot judge a matrix by whether its numbers look big.


  
Two refinements make it more useful than "area":


  
    
    
  • A negative determinant means the space was flipped over, like a mirror. Area
    cannot really be negative, so the sign is carrying extra information: orientation. Under a
    negative-determinant matrix, a shape that read clockwise now reads anticlockwise.
    • 
    
  • A zero determinant means the area was destroyed. The parallelogram has been
    flattened onto a line — or, if the matrix is bad enough, onto a single point. Nothing that is
    flattened can be un-flattened, and this is the case that matters most.
    • 
  


  
Rank: how many dimensions survive


  
When the determinant is zero, something has collapsed. The rank counts what is left:


  

    
The one idea

    
Rank is the number of dimensions that survive the transformation — equivalently,
    the number of genuinely independent columns the matrix has. A 2×2 matrix with rank 2 keeps the
    plane a plane. Rank 1 squashes it onto a line. Rank 0 squashes it to the origin, and only the
    zero matrix does that.

  


  
Rank is never larger than the smaller shape number. A 1000 × 1000 matrix can have
  exact rank 3, so every output lies in a 3-dimensional slice. Noisy real data, however, is usually
  full rank in the exact algebraic sense while being approximately low rank: a few singular
  directions carry most of its useful structure. Dimensionality reduction, compression, and
  recommender systems exploit that effective low rank.


  
The set of everything a matrix can output has a name: the column space. It follows
  directly from Part 5's column view — since Ax is always a combination of A's columns,
  every possible output is in the span of the columns. So the column space is that span, and
  the rank is its dimension. The vocabulary is new; the idea is Part 2's, unchanged.

The inverse: the matrix that undoes


  
If A stretches space, the matrix that puts it back is called A⁻¹, read
  "A inverse". The defining property is exactly what you would want:


  

    A⁻¹A = AA⁻¹ = I
  


  
Do the transformation, then undo it, and you are back where you started. Practice lab F question
  3 already had you build a pair like this: the shear [[1,1],[0,1]] and the opposite
  shear [[1,−1],[0,1]] multiply to the identity, so each is the other's inverse.


  
But an inverse only exists when nothing was destroyed. If the matrix flattened the plane onto a
  line, every point on some vertical line landed in the same place, and no rule could send them back
  where they came from — the information is simply gone. So:


  

    
The equivalence — six ways to say one thing

    
For a square matrix A, the following are all the same statement.
    If any one is true, all are; if any one is false, all are.

    

      
Invertible ("good")Singular ("collapsed")

      
        
A⁻¹ existsA⁻¹ does not exist

        
det(A) ≠ 0det(A) = 0

        
Full rank (rank = n)Rank deficient (rank < n)

        
Columns are linearly independentSome column is a combination of the others

        
Columns span the whole spaceColumns span only a line or plane

        
Ax = b has exactly one solution, for every bAx = b has zero or infinitely many solutions

      
    

    
Do not memorise six facts. Memorise one picture — did the transformation flatten
    anything? — and read the six statements off it.

  


  

    
Recipe · the 2×2 inverse, and when to use it

    
       1      [ d  −b ]
A⁻¹ = ───  ·  [        ]
      det     [ −c   a ]

    
Swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant. Notice what happens
    when det = 0: you would divide by zero, and the formula fails — exactly as the
    geometry demands.

    
Worked, for A = [[1, 2], [3, 4]], whose determinant is
    1×4 − 2×3 = −2:

    
swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal:   [[4, −2], [−3, 1]]
divide every entry by det = −2:               [[−2, 1], [1.5, −0.5]]

check:  [[1, 2], [3, 4]] · [[−2, 1], [1.5, −0.5]]
        row 1: 1×(−2) + 2×1.5 = 1     1×1 + 2×(−0.5) = 0
        row 2: 3×(−2) + 4×1.5 = 0     3×1 + 4×(−0.5) = 1
      = [[1, 0], [0, 1]] = I   ✔

    
Use this by hand for 2×2, and essentially never in code. To solve
    Ax = b, write np.linalg.solve(A, b), not
    np.linalg.inv(A) @ b. The solve route is faster and considerably more
    accurate in floating point, and this is standard professional practice rather than a
    micro-optimisation.

  


  

    
Common pitfalls

    
    
      
  • "det = 0 means the matrix is all zeros." No. The matrix
      [[2, 4], [1, 2]] has a determinant of 2·2 − 4·1 = 0 and not a single
      zero entry. The second column is just twice the first, so both columns point along the same
      line and the transformation flattens the plane.
    • 
      
  • "Rank is the size of the matrix." Rank is the number of independent
      directions, always at most min(rows, columns) and frequently far less. Ask "how
      many of these columns tell me something new?", not "how big is this grid?"
    • 
      
  • Computing an inverse to solve a system. Use np.linalg.solve.
      Inverting is slower, less accurate, and impossible for non-square matrices — where least
      squares (Part 8) still gives you a usable answer.
    • 
      
  • Expecting an exact zero determinant in floating point. A truly singular
      matrix often computes to det ≈ 1e−17 rather than 0, so
      if det == 0 is an unreliable test. Prefer np.linalg.matrix_rank, or
      check the condition number, which measures how close to collapse you are.
    • 
      
  • Judging "nearly singular" from determinant magnitude alone. A small
      determinant is not a scale-free warning: changing units can make a well-conditioned matrix's
      determinant tiny. Judge numerical stability with the condition number, or the ratio
      of largest to smallest singular value. A large condition number means tiny data changes can
      produce large answer changes; in regression this is the danger behind multicollinearity.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Where this shows up in machine learning

    
    
      
  • Multicollinearity. Add a "height in cm" column to a dataset that already
      has "height in inches" and the two columns become exactly dependent. XᵀX becomes
      singular, the regression has no unique solution, and the fitted coefficients become
      meaningless — you can trade weight between the two columns freely. Modules 12 and 13.
    • 
      
  • Why Ridge regression works. Ridge solves
      (XᵀX + λI)w = Xᵀy. Adding λI nudges the matrix away from singular,
      which is why Ridge stabilises exactly the situations described above. That + λI
      is not a hack — it is a direct fix to a rank problem. Module 13.
    • 
      
  • Low rank as a resource. Many real data matrices are well approximated at
      low rank, meaning much of their content is redundant. PCA, image compression, and recommender
      systems exploit this directly. LoRA makes a related assumption: the task-specific update to a
      model's weights can be learned at low rank. Modules 25 and 41.
    • 
      
  • Reading the error message. When NumPy raises
      LinAlgError: Singular matrix, you now know precisely what happened: the columns
      were dependent, the transformation collapsed, and there is no unique answer to be had.
    •

      independent columns, and it multiplies areas by 10.

      
2. 2·2 − 4·1 = 0. Rank 1: the second column
      (4, 2) is exactly twice the first (2, 1), so both point along one
      line and every output lands on that line. The plane is flattened onto the line through
      (2, 1), and the transformation cannot be undone.

      
3. It doubles areas, and it flips orientation — a mirror as well as a
      stretch. The magnitude gives the scaling, the sign gives the flip.

      
4. The inverse is [[0.5, 0], [0, 0.25]]. In words: the matrix
      doubles x and quadruples y, so to undo it you halve x and quarter y. Diagonal matrices are the
      one case where the inverse is obvious — invert each diagonal entry — and it also shows why a
      zero on the diagonal is fatal.

      
5. No unique solution. Rank 4 in a 5-dimensional space means one dimension
      was destroyed: the matrix flattens 5-D space into a 4-D slice. So either b lies in
      that slice, and infinitely many x map to it (you can move freely along the
      destroyed direction without changing the output), or b is outside the slice and
      nothing maps to it at all. Zero solutions or infinitely many — never exactly one.

      noise rather than by your data. The formal name for how bad this is is the condition

Solving Ax = b, and least squares

  linear regression — not the code for it, the reason for it — several modules before you

A system of equations is a matrix equation


  
Here is a pair of equations of the kind you may have met at school:


  
2x +  y = 5
 x + 3y = 10


  
Watch it turn into linear algebra. The coefficients form a matrix, the unknowns form a vector,
  the right-hand sides form another vector:


  

    2113
    xy
    =
    510
     i.e. 
    Ax = b
  


  
Check that this is the same thing: the first row of the product is
  (2, 1) · (x, y) = 2x + y, which must equal 5. It is the same pair of equations,
  rewritten. And Ax = b is the single most common equation in applied mathematics.


  
Two pictures of the same question


  
Because Ax has a row reading and a column reading (Part 5), Ax = b has
  two pictures. Gilbert Strang opens his famous MIT course with exactly this contrast, and it is
  worth the same emphasis here.


  

    

      The row picture — "where do the lines cross?"
      
Each equation is a line in the plane. 2x + y = 5 is one line;
      x + 3y = 10 is another. A solution is a point on both lines — so solving
      means finding the intersection.

      
Familiar from school, and it stops helping in higher dimensions: with 50 unknowns you would
      need to picture 50 intersecting 49-dimensional surfaces.

    

    

      The column picture — "what is the recipe?"
      
Read Ax as a combination of A's columns. The question becomes: how much of
      column 1 and how much of column 2 do I need to add up to reach b?

      
x·(2, 1) + y·(1, 3) = (5, 10). Now it is a question about arrows, and it keeps
      working in any number of dimensions. This is the picture that generalises.

Three things that can happen


  
Every system of linear equations, of any size, falls into exactly one of three cases — and Part
  7 already told you how to tell which.


  

    
CaseRow pictureColumn pictureWhen

    
      

        Exactly one solution
        The lines cross at one point
        The columns are independent, so there is exactly one recipe reaching b
        A is invertible: det ≠ 0, full rank
      

      

        No solution
        The lines are parallel and never meet
        The columns span only a line, and b is off that line — unreachable
        det = 0 and b is outside the column space
      

      

        Infinitely many
        The two lines are the same line
        The columns span a line and b is on it — many recipes reach it
        det = 0 and b is inside the column space
      

    
  


  
Note what is not on this list: "exactly two solutions". Linear systems never have two,
  or seven. If you can find two different solutions you can average them, or extend the line through
  them, and get infinitely many. That rigidity is what "linear" buys you.


  
Solving by elimination — and what to do in practice


  
The systematic hand method is Gaussian elimination: add multiples of one equation to
  another to knock out variables, until one equation has a single unknown, then substitute
  backwards.


  
2x +  y = 5          (1)
 x + 3y = 10         (2)

Take (1) − 2×(2):    2x + y − 2x − 6y = 5 − 20
                                  −5y = −15
                                    y = 3

Substitute back into (1):   2x + 3 = 5  →  x = 1

Solution: (x, y) = (1, 3)      Check: 1 + 3(3) = 10 ✔


  
You should do this by hand a few times so the process is not mysterious — and then essentially
  never again. In code you write np.linalg.solve(A, b), which uses a numerically careful
  version of the same idea. Hand elimination is a concept to understand, not a skill to drill; the
  concepts that repay drilling are the column picture and the three cases.

When there is no solution: least squares


  
Now the situation that actually occurs in machine learning. You have 500 houses and 3 features,
  and you want weights w such that Xw = y — every price predicted exactly.
  That is 500 equations and 3 unknowns.


  
There is no solution. There is essentially never a solution. 500 equations cannot all be
  satisfied by 3 numbers unless the data is unnaturally cooperative, and real data has noise. In the
  column picture: X has 3 columns, so everything it can produce lies in a
  3-dimensional slice of 500-dimensional space, and y is somewhere else entirely.


  

    
The one idea — and it is the whole of linear regression

    
If you cannot reach y, go to the closest point you can reach.
    The set of reachable points is the column space of X. The closest point in it is the
    projection of y onto that space — Part 4's shadow, one dimension up. The
    recipe for that closest point is the least-squares solution ŵ, and the leftover
    arrow y − Xŵ is the residual: the part of the truth your model
    structurally cannot express.

  


  
This is why the answer is a projection and not a compromise. And it explains the defining
  property of least squares, which otherwise looks like a coincidence: the residual is
  perpendicular to every column of X. If it were not — if the residual
  still leaned along some feature direction — you could move a little further in that direction and
  get closer, so you would not have been at the closest point. Perpendicularity is the signature of
  "as close as possible".

Key formula · the normal equations

    
Setting "the residual is perpendicular to every column" into symbols gives
    Xᵀ(y − Xŵ) = 0, which rearranges to:

    
XᵀX ŵ = Xᵀy

if X has independent columns:    ŵ = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀ y

    
The first line is the normal equation and always describes an ordinary
    least-squares optimum. The inverse form requires full column rank, so that XᵀX is
    invertible. In code use np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None): it is more stable than
    forming the inverse and also returns a solution when columns are dependent.

  


  
The rearrangement is three ordinary steps, not a leap. Starting from "the residual is
  perpendicular to every column", which means dotting it with each column of X gives
  zero — and stacking those dot products is exactly what Xᵀ times a vector does:


  
Xᵀ(y − Xŵ) = 0        the residual is perpendicular to every column
Xᵀy − XᵀXŵ = 0        multiply out, exactly as with ordinary numbers
       XᵀXŵ = Xᵀy      move the second term to the other side


  
Stop there. That last line is an Ax = b system with square matrix
  XᵀX, unknown ŵ, and right-hand side Xᵀy. When the columns are
  independent you could solve it directly; when they are dependent it is singular and a
  least-squares solver or pseudoinverse selects one minimiser. The inverse form appears in textbooks
  under the full-rank assumption, but it is the worse way to compute the answer (Part 7's advice:
  solve, do not invert).


  
Worked all the way through, with three points


  
Fit y = mx + c to (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5). No
  line passes through all three — the first two suggest a slope of 1, the last two a slope of 2 — so
  least squares it is. Build X with a column of 1s so the model is allowed an
  intercept:

      [3  1]            [5]

      [1 1 1] [2 1]     [1+2+3   1+1+1]     [ 6   3]
              [3 1]

The line misses every single point, which is the normal and correct outcome. And the residuals
  sum to exactly zero — not a coincidence, but the perpendicularity condition applied to the column
  of 1s. You will confirm all of this in NumPy in the notebook, in one line, and get the same
  numbers.


  
Look at what XᵀX being singular would mean: two features linearly dependent, no
  unique answer, exactly Part 7's collapse. Multicollinearity is not a separate topic — it is this
  formula meeting a rank-deficient matrix. And Ridge regression's (XᵀX + λI) is the
  minimal repair.


  

    
Common pitfalls

    
    
      
  • "Least squares finds a line through the points." It finds the line
      minimising the total squared residual — usually passing through none of them. The
      objective is ‖Xw − y‖², a squared length, exactly as in Part 3.
    • 
      
  • Squaring makes outliers loud. A residual of 10 contributes 100 while a
      residual of 1 contributes 1, so a single bad point can drag the whole line. Try the "outlier"
      preset in Demo 13. This is a real weakness of least squares and the motivation for robust
      alternatives.
    • 
      
  • Forgetting the intercept. A matrix always fixes the origin (Part 5), so
      Xw alone can only produce lines through (0, 0). To allow an intercept
      you append a column of 1s to X — that constant column is what buys you the
      + b. Most libraries do this for you, which is why forgetting it is easy.
    • 
      
  • Inverting instead of solving. np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y
      is the formula transcribed literally, and it is the slow, inaccurate way. Use
      np.linalg.lstsq.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Where this shows up in machine learning

    
    
      
  • Linear regression is this and nothing else. Module 11 fits a line, Module
      12 fits many features at once; both look for the w that makes
      ‖Xw − y‖² — the squared length of the error vector — as small as it can be.
      (Textbooks compress that into ŵ = argmin ‖Xw − y‖²; argmin just means
      "the input that minimises this", as opposed to the minimum value itself.) When you call
      LinearRegression().fit(X, y), scikit-learn solves the equation on this page.
    • 
      
  • Gradient descent is the other route to the same answer. Module 12 walks
      downhill toward the minimum instead of solving directly. Both find the projection; the direct

Eigenvectors, eigenvalues, and a first look at PCA


  
One last question about transformations, and it is the one that unlocks dimensionality
  reduction, recommender systems, and the internals of Google's original search algorithm:
  when a matrix moves everything, is there anything it does not knock off course?


  
The vectors that stay on their own line


  
Go back to Demo 8 and picture a shear. Almost every arrow gets rotated as well as stretched — it
  ends up pointing somewhere new. But some arrows are special: the transformation stretches or
  shrinks them while leaving them on the same line through the origin. Those are the
  eigenvectors, and the factor by which each one is stretched is its
  eigenvalue.


  

    
Key formula · the eigenvector equation

    
A v = λ v

    
Read it aloud: "applying the matrix to v does the same thing as multiplying
    v by a single number." The left side is a full matrix transformation; the right
    side is a plain scaling. For most vectors these are wildly different; for an eigenvector they
    coincide. λ (lambda) is the eigenvalue.

  


  
The German prefix eigen- means "own" or "characteristic" — these are the matrix's
  own directions, the ones intrinsic to it rather than to your choice of axes. What the
  eigenvalue tells you:


  

    
Eigenvalue λWhat happens along that direction

    
      
λ > 1Stretched away from the origin. Repeated application makes it grow.

      
λ = 1Completely unchanged — a fixed direction.

      
0 < λ < 1Shrunk toward the origin. Repeated application makes it vanish.

      
λ = 0Crushed to the origin. This direction is destroyed — the matrix is singular.

      
λ < 0Flipped to the opposite side, and scaled by |λ|. Still the same line.

Why anyone cares: repeated application


  
Eigenvectors would be a curiosity if matrices were applied once. They matter because matrices
  get applied over and over — every year of a population model, every iteration of a
  training loop, every layer of a deep network.


  
For a diagonalizable matrix with one eigenvalue whose absolute value is strictly
  largest, write the starting vector as a combination of eigenvectors. Each application scales every
  component by its own λ. After repeated application and normalisation, any start with a
  nonzero component along the dominant eigenvector approaches that eigenvector's line. If the
  largest magnitudes tie, the matrix lacks a full eigenbasis, or the dominant eigenvalues are
  complex, the iterates need not settle to one direction. Demo 15 uses the well-behaved case.

  eigenvalue. Initialisation, normalisation, gating, and residual connections help keep those
  products well scaled (Module 31).


  
Symmetric matrices are the well-behaved ones


  
A matrix is symmetric if Aᵀ = A — it is a mirror image across its
  diagonal, so A₁₂ = A₂₁. Symmetric matrices have two properties that make them the
  nicest objects in linear algebra:


  
    
    
  • Their eigenvalues are always real — no complex numbers, no rotation, no
    missing eigenvectors.
    • 
    
  • You can always find a full set of perpendicular eigenvectors — so they give
    you a perfectly clean set of new axes to describe your data in.
    • 
  


  
Take care with the exact wording of the second one, because the loose version of it is false.
  Eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are automatically perpendicular. When two
  eigenvalues happen to be equal there is a whole plane of eigenvectors to choose from, and not every
  choice is perpendicular — the identity matrix is symmetric and, as Checkpoint 9 will point out,
  every vector is one of its eigenvectors. The guarantee is that a perpendicular set always
  exists and that np.linalg.eigh will hand you one. That is all PCA needs.


  
This matters because the single most important matrix in data analysis is symmetric. First, a
  word we have not needed until now: the variance of a column of numbers is the average
  squared distance of its values from their mean — one number saying how spread out that feature is.
  Small variance means every example has roughly the same value; large variance means they differ a
  lot. (Module 06 does this properly; this sentence is all Part 9 requires.)


  
Now centre your data — subtract each column's mean, so every feature is measured as a deviation
  from its own average — and compute C = XᵀX / (n − 1). The result is the
  covariance matrix. Its diagonal entries are the variances of the individual features;
  its off-diagonal entry (i, j) says whether features i and j tend to
  be above their averages together (positive), in opposition (negative), or have little linear
  association (near zero). Near-zero covariance means uncorrelated, not necessarily
  independent. Since (XᵀX)ᵀ = XᵀX, it is symmetric by construction — so the guarantee above
  applies, and a perpendicular set of eigenvectors is always available. One more fact, used below:
  the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix add up to the total variance in the data,
  which is what lets you talk about the percentage each direction explains.


  
PCA: the directions your data actually varies in


  

    
The one idea

    
PCA finds the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The top one points along
    the direction your data spreads out most; the next points along the most spread remaining, at
    right angles to the first; and so on. Their eigenvalues say how much variance each direction
    carries. Keep the top few and you have described your data with far fewer numbers.

    Looking ahead The SVD — the one decomposition that always works
    

      
Eigenvectors have a limitation: they need a square matrix, and even then real ones may not
      exist (the rotation preset in Demo 14). Data matrices are almost never square. The
      singular value decomposition fixes both problems, and it applies to
      every matrix without exception:

      
A = U Σ Vᵀ

      
In pictures: every matrix is an orthogonal change of coordinates, axis-aligned
      scaling, then another orthogonal change of coordinates. The orthogonal steps may
      include reflections, and for a rectangular matrix Σ also changes dimension.
      Σ holds the singular
      values, sorted largest first: how much stretch happens along each direction. (Yes, that
      is the same capital sigma as the summation sign from Part 1. The letter is badly overloaded;
      here it names a matrix, which you can tell because it is sitting between two other
      matrices.)

      
The consequence that matters is the one from Part 6's fourth reading of matrix
      multiplication. The SVD rewrites A as a sum of simple layers, ordered by
      importance:

      
A = σ₁·(layer 1) + σ₂·(layer 2) + σ₃·(layer 3) + …

      
Keep only the first few layers and you get the best possible approximation of
      A using that little information. Applied to a photograph, that is image
      compression — a 1000×1000 image kept to 50 layers stores 10% of the numbers and usually looks
      fine. Applied to a users-by-films ratings table, it is a recommender system. Applied to
      centred data, it is PCA — the same answer by a better-behaved route, which is why
      real PCA implementations call an SVD rather than an eigen-solver. Low-rank adaptation uses a
      related factorisation idea: LoRA learns two small matrices whose product is a weight update;
      it does not truncate the original model weights (Module 41).

      
You do not need to compute an SVD by hand, now or ever. You need to recognise the shape of
      the idea: a matrix is a stack of patterns, sorted by importance, and you can throw the
      unimportant ones away. Module 25 makes this concrete, and the second half of the
      notebook's mini-project lets you run it today on a grid of handwritten digits — watching the
      picture reassemble as you keep more layers, and seeing for yourself how much you can discard
      before it stops being readable.

    

  


  

    
Common pitfalls with eigenvectors

    
    
      
  • "An eigenvector is one specific vector." If v is an
      eigenvector then so is 5v and −v — the whole line qualifies. Libraries
      return a normalised one with an arbitrary sign, so your PCA plot may come out
      mirrored compared with a classmate's. Nothing is wrong.
    • 
      
  • "Every matrix has eigenvectors." A pure rotation has no real ones —
      nothing stays on its own line. The eigenvalues turn complex, and complex eigenvalues
      mean rotation. This is a fact about the geometry, not a failure of the method.
    • 
      
  • Forgetting to centre before PCA. If you do not subtract the column means,
      the first component tends to point at the data's mean rather than along its variation, and the
      result is meaningless. Centring is not optional.
    • 
      
  • Forgetting to scale. PCA follows variance, and variance follows units. A
      feature measured in millimetres will dominate one measured in metres purely because its numbers
      are bigger. Standardise features first unless they are genuinely comparable.
    • 
      
  • "PC1 is my most important original feature." Principal components are
      new axes — mixtures of all your features. PC1 is a direction, not a column, which is
      why PCA costs you interpretability.
    • 
      
  • Hunting for a hand method. There is one — you solve an equation written
      det(A − λI) = 0 — and this course never uses it. It is workable
      for a 2×2 on paper and numerically hopeless beyond about 3×3, which is why real software does
      not use it either. Use np.linalg.eig, or np.linalg.eigh for symmetric
      matrices, which is faster and more accurate. You are not missing a technique you were supposed
      to learn.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Where this shows up in machine learning

    
    
      
  • PCA and dimensionality reduction. Module 25, directly.
    • 
      
  • Exploding and vanishing signals or gradients. Across many layers, singular
      values of products of layer Jacobians determine whether directions grow or die — motivating
      careful initialisation and architectures such as residual networks and LSTMs. Modules 31 and
      35.
    • 
      
  • Spectral clustering. Build a similarity matrix between points, take its
      leading eigenvectors, and cluster in that space — it finds groups that k-means cannot. Module
      24.
    • 
      
  • Low-rank everything. Recommender systems, topic modelling, and image
      compression keep a few dominant components; LoRA instead learns a compact low-rank update.
      Both exploit the fact that useful matrix structure often needs far fewer parameters than a
      dense matrix. Module 41.

Glossary


  

    
Scalar
A single number. It scales vectors.

    
Vector
An ordered list of numbers; equivalently a point, or an arrow from the origin.

    
Matrix
A grid of numbers. Either a table of data, or a machine that transforms vectors.

    
Shape
Rows × columns, always in that order.

    
Transpose (Aᵀ)
The matrix flipped across its diagonal; rows become columns.

    
Linear combination
a·u + b·v — scale some vectors and add them up.

    
Span
Every point reachable by linear combinations of a given set of vectors.

    
Linearly independent
No vector in the set is a combination of the others — nothing is redundant.

    
Basis
A set of independent vectors that spans the whole space; coordinates are the coefficients on a basis.

    
Norm (‖v‖)
The length of a vector. L2 is the straight-line length; L1 is the sum of absolute values.

    
Unit vector
A vector of length 1 — pure direction, obtained by dividing by the norm.

    
Dot product (u · v)
Multiply matching entries and add. Measures alignment; equals ‖u‖‖v‖cos θ.

    
Orthogonal
Perpendicular directions with dot product zero. This geometric fact does not by itself imply statistical independence.

    
Cosine similarity
For two nonzero vectors, the dot product with both lengths divided out. Between −1 and 1; undefined for a zero vector.

    
Projection
The shadow one vector casts on another — the closest point on that line or subspace.

    
Linear transformation
A mapping that keeps grid lines straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and fixes the origin.

    
Identity (I)
The matrix that changes nothing. AI = IA = A.

    
Determinant
The factor by which a matrix scales area or volume. Negative means orientation flipped; zero means collapsed.

    
Rank
How many dimensions survive the transformation — the number of independent columns.

    
Column space
The span of a matrix's columns; everything the matrix can possibly output.

    
Singular
Not invertible. Determinant zero, rank deficient, information destroyed.

    
Inverse (A⁻¹)
The matrix that undoes A. Exists only when nothing was destroyed.

    
Least squares
The best available answer when Ax = b has no solution: project b onto the column space.

    
Residual
y − ŷ, the part of the truth the model could not express. Always orthogonal to the column space.

    
Eigenvector
A direction the matrix does not knock off its own line — it only stretches it.

    
Eigenvalue (λ)
How an eigen-direction is scaled. Repetition grows its magnitude when |λ| > 1, shrinks it when |λ| < 1, and preserves it when |λ| = 1; a negative value also flips direction each step.

    
Symmetric
Aᵀ = A. Guarantees real eigenvalues, and that a perpendicular set of eigenvectors can always be found.

    
Variance
The average squared distance of a feature's values from their mean — how spread out one column is.

    
Subspace
A flat slice of a space that passes through the origin: a line inside a plane, or a plane inside 3-D space. A column space is a subspace.

    
Condition number
How close a matrix is to collapsing. Large means "nearly singular": the answer is unstable and dominated by rounding and noise.

    
Regularisation
Adding a penalty that favours smaller weights, reducing sensitivity to noise and the risk of overfitting. Ridge penalises ‖w‖², Lasso penalises ‖w‖₁. Module 13.

    
Overdetermined
More equations than unknowns — the normal situation with real data, and the reason least squares exists.

    
Covariance matrix
XᵀX/(n−1) on centred data — how features vary together. Always symmetric.

    
PCA
Finding the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix: the directions of greatest variance.

    
SVD
A = UΣVᵀ. Orthogonal coordinate changes around axis-aligned scaling, with a possible dimension change. Works for every rectangular or square matrix.
